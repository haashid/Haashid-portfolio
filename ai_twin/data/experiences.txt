## WORK EXPERIENCES & STORIES

## Story 1: Building Under Hackathon Pressure

Situation:
I've participated in multiple hackathons and technical competitions where teams have to go from an idea to a working prototype within a very limited amount of time.

Task:
My responsibility was to contribute to the technical solution while making sure the team focused on building something functional rather than trying to implement too many features.

Action:
I approached these situations by first understanding the problem and identifying the core user workflow.

Instead of trying to build the entire product immediately, I focused on the most technically important components first. I broke the system into smaller pieces, identified risky technical areas, validated those early, and then built the surrounding features.

I also had to learn technologies quickly when the project required something unfamiliar.

Result:
This approach contributed to multiple successful hackathon outcomes, including Grand Champion at VEXSTORM 2026, National Runner-Up at HackToFuture 4.0, 2nd Runner-Up at the East India Blockchain Summit 2.0, and 2nd Place at Hackotsava 2025.

What I learned:
Speed is important in hackathons, but prioritization is even more important. A smaller working system is usually more valuable than a large unfinished one.

## Story 3: Conducting Workshops for 300+ Students

Situation:
Many students understand technology better when they can physically build and experiment rather than only listen to a lecture.

Task:
I took part in conducting practical technical workshops designed to make engineering more hands-on and accessible.

Action:
I worked on workshop activities involving practical engineering and hardware. One notable example was the two-day BUILDX RC Car workshop at BIT Polytechnic, where I served as a resource person.

The workshop involved Arduino-based RC cars, programming, wiring, assembly, and debugging.

Result:
Across my technical workshops, I have reached 300+ students.

I was also featured as a resource person by Vartha Bharathi for the workshop.

What I learned:
Explaining something to others forces you to understand it more deeply yourself. Technical knowledge becomes much more valuable when you can make it understandable to someone else.

## Story 4: Recovering When a Technical Approach Doesn't Work

Situation:
While working on projects and hackathons, I've encountered situations where an initial technical approach wasn't practical, reliable, or achievable within the available time.

Task:
The responsibility was to avoid getting stuck on the original implementation and still deliver a working solution.

Action:
I learned to identify the actual requirement behind a feature and then look for a simpler way to satisfy it.

Instead of becoming attached to the first implementation, I evaluate the trade-offs, reduce unnecessary complexity, and prioritize the core functionality.

Result:
This mindset has helped me continue delivering working prototypes under time constraints and across different technical domains.

What I learned:
A good engineer should be attached to the problem, not to a particular implementation.

---

## Story 5: Working Across Different Technical Domains

Situation:
My projects have covered very different areas, including AI, blockchain, IoT, cybersecurity, audit technology, recruitment, and meeting intelligence.

Task:
Each project required learning a different combination of technologies and understanding a different problem domain.

Action:
I approached each project by first understanding the problem and architecture before selecting the implementation approach.

For example, my work has included AI-based systems such as TrueHire AI and ORION, blockchain projects such as Weilchain Nexus and RakshaChain, and IoT systems such as the Real-Time Heart Health Monitoring System.

Result:
This has given me broad technical exposure and made me comfortable moving between different technology stacks when a problem requires it.

What I learned:
Technologies change quickly. The more important skill is being able to understand systems and learn the technology required to build them.
